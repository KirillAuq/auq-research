Key findings

What we found

  • Homepages know what matters. 94 of 100 make at least one of the four promises engineering leaders are hired to own somewhere on the page.
  • Most don’t lead with it. Only 35 of 100 put one of the four promises in the headline. The other 59 have the line further down and lead with something else.
  • Only 6 of 100 headlines give the buyer a concrete promise: a number, a time frame, or another metric the buyer could hold the company to.
  • All four promises get buried the same way. Architecture and stack fit is on 82 homepages and in 9 headlines.
  • It depends on the category. When the product solves the buyer’s problem directly, the headline says so: 9 of 12 observability homepages and 9 of 12 testing homepages lead with one of the four promises. Auth (0 of 12) and application security (0 of 13) never do; they lead with security, which we didn’t score as one of the four.

Why we ran it

The headline is the first impression

When a company buys a dev tool, an engineering leader is usually in the decision. Your homepage headline is often the first thing they read. If it doesn’t speak to what they are accountable for, you’ve lost the first impression, even when the proof sits a few scrolls down.

Why engineering leaders buy dev tools

Engineering leaders buy dev tools to hit the goals they’re measured on. Their job posts spell out what those goals are. Four of the responsibilities that came up most are ones a tool can directly help with. When a homepage says it will help with one of them, that is what we call a promise.

What concrete looks like

Could a buyer hold you to it?

Most headlines led with a general claim, a security promise or a plain product description. A few gave the buyer something to verify. We turned real headlines into patterns, so no company can be identified; the bracketed parts are what each company filled in.

Concrete: a buyer could hold you to these

6 of 100 headlines did this.

  • “[N]x cheaper than [a named competitor]”
  • “[A feature] in minutes instead of months”
  • “[A feature] in days, not quarters”
  • “Recover from [an outage] in minutes, not weeks”

Generic: a buyer can’t verify these

The kind of line most headlines led with.

  • “The [category] platform built for the speed of AI”
  • “The [noun] layer for agents and apps”
  • “Ship software [a feeling]”

The rubric

Run it on your own homepage

“Does our homepage speak to the buyer?” usually gets answered by gut feel. This is the rubric we used, so you can apply it the same way every time.

  1. Start with what your buyer is accountable for. For engineering leaders, four things a tool can help with: architecture and stack fit, reliability, quality and delivery speed. Each is a promise your homepage can make.
  2. Read only your headline and the line under it. Which of the four promises does it lead with, if any?
  3. Test that line. “Deploys go from 30 minutes to 3” passes. “Ship 10x faster” doesn’t: faster than what? A multiplier needs a baseline the buyer can check, like a named competitor or a before-and-after.
  4. Read the whole page and mark every promise it makes. Customer quotes and case-study stats don’t count; only your own claims do.
  5. If a stronger promise sits further down the page, that’s the line to test in your headline.

Could a buyer hold you to the promise? If not, it isn’t specific enough.Generic AI lines (“[X] at the speed of AI”) don’t pass on their own. Use them as context, not as the promise.

Two scoring rules that settled the edge cases

  • “Set up in 5 minutes” is about installing the product, so it isn’t delivery speed.
  • Only the company’s own claims count: a vendor-stated number passes, a case-study or testimonial number doesn’t.

How we ran it

Method and limitations

  • Job posts. 50 public LinkedIn job posts for engineering-leadership roles at B2B tech companies, US, posted 17 September to 16 October 2026: VP Engineering (16), Head or Director of Engineering (12), Head of Platform or Infrastructure (11) and Engineering Manager (11). We grouped every responsibility in them into themes, with no preset list.
  • The four promises. Of the themes that came up most, four are promises a dev tool can make: technical strategy and architecture (43 of 50 posts), reliability (39), engineering quality (39) and shipping predictably (33). Hiring and growing the team (48) and partnering with the business (46) came up more often, but no tool can promise those, so they are not scored. Security and compliance (33 of 50 posts) is a promise a tool can make too, but it wasn’t one of the promises we scored; 20 of the 100 headlines led with a security promise.
  • Homepages. 100 dev-tool companies, seed to Series C, 12 or 13 in each of 8 categories. The list was drafted by our team and cross-checked against YC and G2.
  • Definitions. “In the headline” means the headline and the line directly under it. “On the homepage” means anywhere on the homepage. Only claims in the company’s own voice count; customer quotes, testimonials and case-study stats don’t.
  • Concrete promise. A headline counts as concrete when it puts a number or a time frame on what the buyer gets. Aspiration (“the next [N] years”), social proof (“[N]+ teams”), scope (“24/7”) and setup time (“live in minutes”) don’t count.
  • Scoring. Every homepage was scored with Claude against the same written rules, so every page was judged the same way.
  • Limitations. One snapshot of each homepage on one day, US job posts only, a company list we drafted ourselves, and some judgment even with written rules. Results are reported by category, not by company.
